You've found a beautiful period home in an inner-Melbourne suburb. Great bones, charming streetscape, tree-lined street. Then you see it in the planning report: "Heritage Overlay — HO245." Your excitement is suddenly tinged with uncertainty. Can you renovate? Can you extend? Can you knock it down and start again? Here's what heritage overlays actually mean for property owners in Victoria.

## What Is a Heritage Overlay?

A heritage overlay (HO) is a planning control applied to properties, precincts, or places that have been assessed as having heritage significance. It's part of the local council's planning scheme and is legally binding.

Heritage overlays exist at two levels:

**Individual heritage listing** — A specific property identified as having heritage significance in its own right. Think grand Victorian mansions, significant public buildings, or properties associated with notable historical events or people.

**Precinct heritage listing** — A broader area where the collection of buildings, streetscape, and character is considered significant as a whole. This is far more common. Many inner and middle-ring Melbourne suburbs have large heritage precincts covering hundreds of properties, including some that aren't individually remarkable but contribute to the overall character of the area.

A property in a heritage precinct might be classified as:

- **Significant** — Individually important to the heritage character
- **Contributory** — Contributes to the heritage character of the precinct (the most common classification)
- **Non-contributory** — Doesn't contribute to heritage character (usually later additions or replacements within the precinct)

Your classification matters enormously for what you can and can't do.

## What You Can't Do (Without a Permit)

Under a heritage overlay, you generally need a planning permit to:

- **Demolish** the building (or any part of it visible from a public place)
- **Alter the external appearance** of the building (front and sides visible from the street)
- **Build an extension** that's visible from the street
- **Change the front fence** style, height, or materials
- **Remove significant trees** listed in the heritage schedule
- **Construct new buildings** on the site
- **Subdivide** the land
- **Install solar panels** visible from the street (some councils)
- **Change roof materials** or colour (if visible from street)

The key phrase is "visible from a public place." Most councils allow much more freedom with changes to the rear of a property that aren't street-visible.

## What You CAN Usually Do

Heritage overlays aren't as restrictive as most people think. You can typically:

- **Renovate the interior** however you like — heritage overlays almost never control interior modifications
- **Build a rear extension** — As long as it's not visible from the street and is sympathetically designed
- **Update kitchens and bathrooms** — No restrictions on internal fitout
- **Maintain and repair** the building using matching materials
- **Paint** in sympathetic colours (some councils require permits for exterior colour changes, others don't)
- **Add modern amenities** — Heating, cooling, solar panels (on rear roof slopes), updated electrical and plumbing
- **Landscape the rear garden** — Heritage controls rarely apply to back gardens

The general rule is: keep the front and street-visible sides looking heritage, do what you want at the back.

## The Permit Process

If you need a heritage planning permit, here's what to expect:

**Step 1: Pre-application meeting** — Most councils offer free or low-cost pre-application meetings with their heritage advisor. This is worth doing before you spend money on architect drawings. The heritage advisor will tell you what's likely to be approved and what isn't.

**Step 2: Planning application** — Submit plans and a heritage impact statement through your council's planning portal. Include photos of existing conditions, proposed changes, material specifications, and how the proposal responds to heritage guidelines.

**Step 3: Assessment** — The council's heritage advisor assesses your application against the planning scheme and relevant heritage policies. This typically takes 2-4 months for straightforward applications.

**Step 4: Decision** — Council either approves (with or without conditions), requests changes, or refuses the application. You can appeal a refusal to VCAT (Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal).

**Typical costs:**
- Planning permit application: $1,000-$3,000 (council fees)
- Heritage consultant: $2,000-$5,000 (if needed for complex applications)
- Architect familiar with heritage: Essential — budget accordingly
- VCAT appeal (if needed): $2,000-$10,000

**Typical timeframe:** 3-6 months for a standard heritage permit. Complex applications or those requiring VCAT can take 12+ months.

## How Heritage Overlays Affect Property Value

The effect is more nuanced than most people assume:

**Positive effects:**
- Heritage precincts protect neighbourhood character, preventing unsympathetic development
- Tree-lined streets with period homes are consistently among Melbourne's most desirable (and valuable) locations
- Heritage properties attract buyers who appreciate character — often higher-income purchasers
- Heritage controls prevent neighbours from building ugly, out-of-character additions that would affect your amenity

**Negative effects:**
- Renovation costs are typically 15-30% higher due to heritage requirements (matching materials, sympathetic design, permit process)
- You can't demolish and subdivide for multiple dwellings (which limits development potential)
- Heritage permits add time and cost to any external modification
- Some buyers are put off by the perceived restrictions and avoid heritage properties entirely

**The net effect?** Heritage overlays generally support property values in established suburbs. The suburbs with the strongest heritage controls (Fitzroy, Carlton, East Melbourne, South Yarra, Hawthorn) are among Melbourne's most expensive, partly because of the character protection those overlays provide.

## Heritage Overlay Red Flags for Buyers

**Significant classification on a property needing major work** — If a heritage-significant building needs a new roof, repointing, or structural repairs, you must use matching materials and methods, which costs considerably more than modern alternatives.

**Incomplete heritage documentation** — If previous owners have made changes without heritage approval (common), you may inherit enforcement issues. The council can require you to reverse unauthorised works at your cost.

**Heritage trees** — Some heritage schedules include specific trees. If a significant elm or oak is on your property and it's listed, you can't remove it even if it's damaging your foundations. You need a permit even to prune it.

**Mandatory heritage colours** — Some precinct guidelines specify acceptable paint colours and materials. Check before assuming you can paint your Victorian terrace matte black.

## How to Check Heritage Status

**VicPlan** — The Victorian Government's online planning tool (mapshare.vic.gov.au/vicplan) shows all planning overlays including heritage. Enter the address and look for any HO references.

**Council heritage database** — Most councils publish their heritage studies and property gradings (Significant, Contributory, Non-contributory) online.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I knock down a heritage-listed house?**
Almost never for Significant or Contributory properties. Non-contributory properties within a precinct can sometimes be demolished, but you'll still need a permit and the replacement must be sympathetically designed.

**Do heritage rules apply to the interior?**
Almost never. Interior modifications are your business. The only exceptions are properties with specific interior controls, which are extremely rare and only apply to the most significant buildings.

**Will a heritage overlay stop me getting a building permit?**
No — a heritage planning permit is separate from a building permit. You need both for external works. The heritage permit addresses design appropriateness; the building permit addresses compliance with building regulations.

**Can I add a second storey to a heritage house?**
Possibly, if it's set back from the front so it's not visible from the street (or barely visible). Many successful heritage extensions use a "recessive" design — lower than the original roofline, set back significantly, and in contemporary materials that clearly distinguish old from new.
